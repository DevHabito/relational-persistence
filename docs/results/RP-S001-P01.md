## 1. Why this particular question?

The broad motivating question is whether internally organized relations predict how long a system can withstand an external disturbance before losing a specified function. To make it falsifiable, we **must specify the function, disturbance, source population and observation window**. Otherwise “persistence” and “noise” can shift meaning after the result.

We deliberately tested a **limited candidate sufficient statistic**, not all conceivable structural information:

```
X(G) = (number of nodes, number of edges,
        full labeled degree vector,
        minimum source-to-target edge cut)
```

The candidate claim was: `X(G)` plus a uniform, fixed-size two-edge disturbance should determine the probability that terminals remain connected after one disturbance.

This is a stringent falsification target for simple scalar indices, because it conditions on considerably more than total relation count. However, `X(G)` does **not** encode the full adjacency pattern. Finding different persistence rates for equal `X(G)` would therefore **not** show that complete topology is insufficient, or that no enriched structure-and-noise predictor could work.

## 2. Frozen finite experiment and exact outcomes

We exhaustively enumerated all `C(14,8)=3,003` possible labeled simple undirected 6-vertex, 8-edge graphs excluding the direct terminal edge (0,5), retaining those connected across all vertices with both terminal degrees exactly 2 and s–t minimum **edge** cut equal to 2. We found **540 admissible source graphs**.

All `C(8,2)=28` pairs of distinct original edges were removed **without replacement** in every admissible graph. Two independent connectivity computations—BFS and a separate simple-path DFS—and two mincut computations—vertex bipartition cuts and one/two edge removals—were required to agree. No random sampling, fitted parameter, arbitrary numeric tolerance or uncertain significance estimate was used.

| First-run measurement | Exact result |
|---|---:|
| All possible 8-edge source graphs | 3,003 |
| Qualified graphs | **540** |
| Classes with equal **full labeled degree vectors** | 79 |
| Degree classes containing at least two graphs | 55 |
| Classes where the two-edge survival fraction varies | **31** |
| Maximum survival numerator difference within one degree class | 2 of 28 |
| Graphs surviving 24 of 28 edge failures | 24 |
| Graphs surviving 25 of 28 edge failures | 204 |
| Graphs surviving 26 of 28 edge failures | 312 |

The preregistered **first lexicographic witness pair with unequal survival fractions** is:

**Graph A** (eight unordered edges):
```text
(0,1) (0,2) (1,2) (1,3) (1,4) (1,5) (2,3) (4,5)
```

**Graph B**:
```text
(0,1) (0,2) (1,2) (1,3) (1,4) (1,5) (2,5) (3,4)
```

For **both**, the entire labeled degree vector is `(2,5,3,2,2,2)`; source `0` and target `5` each have degree 2, and the minimum source–target **edge** cut is 2. Both remain connected after **every one-edge deletion**. Yet after exactly two random original edges fail, the fraction of the 28 equally likely subsets that preserve the designated terminal route is **25/28** for graph A and **26/28 = 13/14** for graph B.

The exact difference is **1/28**. There is no rounding or statistical uncertainty. The experiment therefore found a finite counterexample to the **declared sufficiency claim**: equal labeled degrees, edge count and terminal minimum cut do **not** uniquely determine that **specific random two-edge-failure** survival fraction.

The result does not imply that these two graphs exhibit different resilience for every failure distribution or function, and it cannot be generalized from six vertices to the natural world without independent evidence.

## 3. Why the disturbance's temporal history also matters

We independently registered two **three-observation** failure processes applied to each same graph. At **every individual observation**, the failure distribution is **identical** in the two processes: exactly two of the same eight original edges are absent, selected uniformly from 28 unordered sets.

- **Frozen failures:** choose one two-edge set at time 1 and keep it throughout all three observations.
- **Independent renewed failures:** redraw a fresh uniformly distributed two-edge failure set at each observation.

Let `K` denote the number of surviving two-edge subsets in that graph. The probability of retaining a source–target path **at every observation** is `K/28` in the frozen model, and `(K/28)^3` in the independent model. The latter was independently checked by enumerating all **28³ = 21,952** ordered three-step failure schedules, without Monte Carlo.

| Graph | One-time probability | Three observations, frozen failures | Three observations, independently renewed failures |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | **25/28** | **25/28** | **15,625/21,952** |
| B | **26/28 = 13/14** | **13/14** | **2,197/2,744** |

Identical **per-observation failure marginals** and the same original topology do **not** imply the same **sustained three-observation outcome** when temporal failure correlations differ. This is an elementary fact about dependent stochastic events, not new statistical mechanics.

It is crucial that “three observations” here is an externally imposed experimental horizon. We **did not derive the existence, direction or emergence of physical time** from these computations.

## 5. Prior literature: which ideas were already known?

These results fit firmly inside **existing network reliability theory**. They are not a novel general discovery that “relations matter.”

1. **Menger, Karl (1927),** classical connectivity theorem. Minimum cut and disjoint paths describe worst-case fault tolerance. See [Graph Connectivity](https://mediatum.ub.tum.de/doc/1094497/TUM-I0422.pdf), Theorems 2.2–2.4, including the later edge-connectivity formulation. We have used a special finite version of these established concepts, not proved a new version of Menger.
2. **Albert, Réka; Jeong, Hawoong; Barabási, Albert-László (2000),** “Error and attack tolerance of complex networks,” *Nature* 406, pp. 378–382, DOI [10.1038/35019019](https://doi.org/10.1038/35019019). Distinguished robustness to errors from vulnerability to targeted attacks.
3. **Brown, Jason I.; Kolokolnikov, Theodore; Kooij, Robert E. (2024),** “New approximations for network reliability,” *Networks* 84(1), pp. 51–63, DOI [10.1002/net.22215](https://doi.org/10.1002/net.22215). Explicitly studies random edge removal, graph reliability, limitations of degree-based approximations, and all- versus two-terminal reliability.
4. **Robustness and resilience of complex networks** (2024), *Nature Reviews Physics* 6, pp. 114–131, DOI [10.1038/s42254-023-00676-y](https://doi.org/10.1038/s42254-023-00676-y). Reviews vulnerability to environmental/network perturbations.
5. **Earlier work in this repository**, particularly **RP-F003-A01** (degree-matched 6-node graphs with different declared consensus residuals) and **RP-F004-A01** (two Markov sign-noise systems with equal marginal noise variance but different stationary output variance), **already exhibited related failures of coarse predictors** in other finite models. Their original result archives and previous claims are unaffected by this study.

The distinct contribution of RP-S001 is a **precisely scoped, preregistered, reproducible finite calculation** using one particular terminal connectivity outcome and two explicitly specified fault-time regimes. This is a legitimate research-process contribution, **not sufficient to claim theoretical priority or novelty in mathematics**. A wider originality search and independent expert review would still be required even for any more ambitious future theorem.

## 6. What does this say about a possible 'hidden law'?

We can now reject a **particular inadequate formulation**:

> A persistence threshold depends only on the number of relations, degree information, and the minimum cut, without requiring the complete topology or a disturbance-time model.

That statement is **false within the registered six-node finite class**. It does **not** refute the possibility of conditional prediction using **complete relational topology, a fully specified perturbation process, a declared function, an observation horizon and an intervention/repair rule**. In fact, these are exactly the missing variables any serious next predictor must address.

A scientifically stronger **next question**, still unresolved and **not answered by the present first run**, is:

> Can a model combining pre-disturbance **complete relational structure**, a measured/known disturbance-time process, and actual available repair actions predict the persistence or restoration of a declared function on genuinely held-out systems **better than** conventional network-reliability and task-specific baselines, under explicitly measured computation and operation costs?

Such a claim becomes potentially interesting only with **out-of-sample prediction**, independent reproducibility, ablations (remove topology, remove autocorrelation, remove repair information), predecision data leakage guards, explicit no-repair and classical reliability baselines, and at least one additional independently observed domain. The real ANEEL D06–D10 work identifies strong textual relationships but still lacks a verified contemporaneous repair-action/treatment record; it **does not authorize this test in real electricity networks yet**.

There is currently **no justified statement that time emerges from noise-handling failure**. Neither three artificial observation ticks nor changes in a probability under temporal autocorrelation derive physical time, even as a mathematical theorem.

**Conclusion:** A candidate coarse sufficiency rule was falsified exactly. The project has a sharper scientific boundary and a reproducible example showing which information cannot simply be omitted. **We do not yet have a scientifically novel universal persistence law**, and finding one remains open.
