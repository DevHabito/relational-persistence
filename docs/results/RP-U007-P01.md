# RP-U007-P01 — Live backlog adds no confirmed increment over an input- and capacity-informed recovery forecast

**17 November 2026, first original trial outcome.** The initial prospective 12-case held-out software experiment produced **`RP_U007_COMPLETE_NO_CONFIRMED_INCREMENT`** under the exact frozen decision criteria. Every original trial was eligible and retained. This negative decision remains intact even though the live-state predictor had a slightly lower mean error.

## Why the U006 finding needed this check

RP-U006 (PR #104) had an 88.0% reduction in its held-out recovery-time error when measured outstanding work was compared with a baseline that only knew worker availability and the median historical recovery time. That was a valid *limited* software result. But every job in that study was injected by the experimenter and therefore its workload was not hidden. A conventional queue-aware forecaster would have been substantially stronger than a constant baseline.

RP-U007 held the richer baseline to the same regime-aware information used by the live-state predictor: initial jobs, exact early acknowledged arrivals, the time since re-enabling service, expected future arrivals, and a separate measured capacity interval. **The sole difference** was one recent live-service `accepted-completed` reading. The question was not whether queues matter in general (they do), but whether one extra live snapshot adds useful predictive accuracy **conditional on input and capacity information already known**.

This comparison is squarely within the existing literature of arrival-aware, queue-length and history-based wait forecasts; it is not a new estimator class or a claim of scientific priority.

## Source measurement and prediction identity

This was a controlled localhost `asyncio` software worker with externally timed input batches, three disclosed per-regime service-time distributions and private per-job OS-random nominal delay draws. The service and read-only health monitor ran as distinct operating-system processes **on the same VM**. The monitor used service-reported counters; it was **not independent hardware instrumentation**.

The three separately source-measured median completion intervals were:

| Regime | Registered requested per-job duration options | Median observed consecutive completion interval |
|---|---|---:|
| fast | 6, 9, 12 ms | **9.587 ms** |
| standard | 8, 12, 16 ms | **12.503 ms** |
| slow | 12, 18, 24 ms | **18.829 ms** |

Both forecast models used the **correct disclosed regime**. At +100ms after the re-enabled worker's actual server timestamp, the first baseline calculated `q_input = max(0, N_accepted - floor(0.100 / measured_regime_interval))`. The second replaced only that workload estimate with **one recent, qualifying observed health snapshot** (`q_state = accepted - completed`), strictly before the future +160ms injection. Both predictions then used the same `max((q+2)×interval, 0.060) + 0.024` seconds. The extra two arrivals at +160ms were anticipated equally by both methods; only their actual timestamp remained unknown before arrival.

The target was the second of two post-final-injection monitor zero-outstanding samples, relative to the +100ms landmark. Two source event and monitor role logs and their server/client timestamps were independently replayed, including all accepted, started, completed job identities, queue conservation and original pre-future prediction files. **544 original monitor samples** were retained across the 15 trials. A source-invalid trial would have invalidated the entire original cohort, but **no trial was excluded**.

## What the result does and does not establish

Within these 12 designed software cases, adding one real-time queue reading to an already informed **arrival-plus-capacity** predictor did not satisfy the declared increment criterion. It is **not a proof** that direct backlog measurements never add information, nor a statistically powered null effect. The sample is one program, one VM, a fixed batch schedule, one worker, three internally imposed nominal duration regimes, and deterministic trial ordering. The monitor is a second process reading the service's own counters, not a separate physical instrument. Distinct jobs from the same program are not independent natural systems.

This study helps **bound the interpretation of the earlier U006 success**: much of the advantage of measured queue state over a constant baseline may be recovered by giving the baseline ordinary workload and service-capacity information. RP-U007's near-equality in seven cases is compatible with this mechanism, but cannot apportion every error to program design, timing jitter or sampling uncertainty.

No physical repair of a network link or two-route graph was performed; neither experiment establishes neural persistence, identity as a dissipative structure, cancer remission or a new physical/biological law. No new Lean theorem or verified scheduler model is claimed. Mathematical queues and input-aware waiting-time forecasts already have extensive prior work.

A stronger next study must test an **actual, independently instrumented functional output**, preferably on new hardware or independent public service traces with unknown arrival/service variability. It should compare the proposed state variable against established input-history and capacity-aware predictors — not against an artificially uninformed constant. This original negative decision should remain accessible to reviewers, regardless of any future positive findings.
